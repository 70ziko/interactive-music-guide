Pitch is frequency, heard. This lesson turns that into the numbers Strudel uses, and explains why some note combinations sound smooth and others tense.

## The octave: ×2

Double a frequency and you get **the same note, one octave higher**. A3 = 220 Hz, A4 = 440 Hz, A5 = 880 Hz. Every harmonic of the low A lines up with a harmonic of the high A, so the ear hears them as "the same note".

Western music splits each octave into **12 equal steps** called semitones. Equal means each step multiplies the frequency by the same number:

<Formula>one semitone = ×2^(1/12) ≈ ×1.0595</Formula>

Twelve of those multiply to exactly ×2. Give every key a number (the MIDI number: A4 = 69, C4 = 60) and the frequency of any note is:

<Formula>f = 440 · 2^((m − 69) / 12)</Formula>

## Intervals are ratios

An **interval** is the distance between two notes. Musically you count semitones. Physically it is a **ratio** of frequencies.

| Interval | Semitones | Pure ratio | Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unison | 0 | 1 : 1 | same note |
| Octave | 12 | 2 : 1 | same note, higher |
| Fifth | 7 | 3 : 2 | strong, open, "power" |
| Fourth | 5 | 4 : 3 | open, a bit hanging |
| Major third | 4 | 5 : 4 | bright, happy |
| Minor third | 3 | 6 : 5 | dark, moody — the default in most dance music |
| Tritone | 6 | 45 : 32 | tense, unstable |

**Simpler ratio → smoother sound.** With 3 : 2, every 3rd cycle of the upper note lines up with every 2nd cycle of the lower note, and many of their harmonics land on the same frequencies. With 45 : 32 they almost never line up, and the ear hears friction.

<Callout kind="warn">
**A note is not a ratio.** C is just a frequency (C4 ≈ 261.6 Hz). A ratio like 3 : 2 describes the *distance between two notes* — a fifth, for example A3 (220 Hz) → E4 (330 Hz). Keep the two ideas apart: notes are points, intervals are distances.
</Callout>

## Rhythm sped up becomes pitch

Your intuition that "notes are sped-up patterns" is right in a precise way. A click repeated 2 times per second is a rhythm. Repeated 200 times per second it is a 200 Hz tone. There is no switch between the two — somewhere around 20 repeats per second the ear stops counting and starts hearing pitch.

So a **3 : 2 polyrhythm** (three hits against two in the same time) sped up past ~20 Hz becomes **a fifth**. The same pattern is rhythm at one speed and harmony at another.

<TryThis>
Pick **3:2**, press Play at 1 cycle/s and count "1-2-3 against 1-2". Slowly drag the speed up. Around 10–20 cycles/s it turns into a buzz. Past ~40 it is two notes a fifth apart. Then try **5:4**: a major third.
</TryThis>

## The small lie of equal temperament

12 equal steps cannot hit the pure ratios exactly. A fifth is 7 semitones = 2^(7/12) = **1.4983**, not 1.5. A major third is 2^(4/12) = **1.2599**, not 1.25.

Musicians measure this in **cents**: 100 cents = 1 semitone.

<Formula>cents = 1200 · log2(ratio)</Formula>

- Equal fifth vs pure fifth: **−2 cents**. Inaudible in practice.
- Equal major third vs pure: **+14 cents**. Audible as a slight shimmer or beating.

Synths, Strudel and every DAW use equal temperament, so you never need to tune by hand. What matters for writing: **octaves and fifths are rock-solid**, thirds are a little rough. That is why basses, hoovers and supersaws stack octaves and fifths for power, and why thirds stay in the mids, never in the bass.

<Strudel title="power: root + fifth + octave" code={`
  note("<a1 f1 c2 g1>")
    .add(note("0,7,12"))   // root, fifth, octave
    .s("sawtooth").lpf(900)
    ._pianoroll()
`} />
