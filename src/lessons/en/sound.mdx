Everything in a track — kick, bass, hoover, vocal — is one thing: **air pressure moving back and forth**. A speaker cone pushes air forward and pulls it back. Your eardrum copies that motion. Three numbers describe it.

| Property | What it is | Unit | You hear it as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** `f` | How many back-and-forth cycles per second | Hz (hertz) | Pitch: low ↔ high |
| **Amplitude** `A` | How far the air moves | 0–1 (or dB) | Loudness |
| **Waveform** | The *shape* of one cycle | — | Tone colour (timbre) |

## One cycle, as math

The simplest sound is a sine wave:

<Formula>y(t) = A · sin(2π · f · t)</Formula>

- `t` is time in seconds.
- `2π · f · t` is how far round the circle you are: one full turn (2π) per cycle, `f` turns per second.
- The **period** `T = 1 / f` is the length of one cycle. 110 Hz → T = 9.09 ms.

Humans hear roughly 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz. Below ~20 Hz you feel pulses rather than pitch — keep that in mind, it comes back in lesson 2.

## Harmonics: why a saw sounds different from a sine

A sawtooth is not a new kind of thing. It is **many sine waves added together**: one at `f`, one at `2f`, one at `3f`, and so on, each quieter. These are the **harmonics** (also called partials or overtones). This is Fourier's idea: any repeating shape = a sum of sines at whole-number multiples of the base frequency.

| Waveform | Which harmonics | How loud is harmonic `n` | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sine | only 1 | — | pure, round, sub-bass |
| Triangle | odd: 1, 3, 5… | 1/n² | soft, flute-like |
| Square | odd: 1, 3, 5… | 1/n | hollow, reedy, "8-bit" |
| Sawtooth | all: 1, 2, 3… | 1/n | bright, buzzy — the raw material of most synth leads, basses and hoovers |

<HarmonicBuilder />

<TryThis>
Start from **sine**. Raise only harmonics 3, 5 and 7. You are building a square wave by hand. Then press **sawtooth** and look: every bar is filled.
</TryThis>

<Callout kind="math">
The base frequency `f` sets the pitch. The harmonics sit at `2f, 3f, 4f…`, so the *spacing* between them is always `f`. Your brain reads that regular spacing as "one note" instead of many. That is why a sawtooth with 50 harmonics still sounds like a single A.
</Callout>

## Hear it in Strudel

Each `<…>` value plays for one cycle, so this steps through the four waveforms on the same note. `._scope()` draws the real output under the line.

<Strudel title="four waveforms, one note" code={`
  note("a2*4")
    .s("<sine triangle square sawtooth>")
    .gain(0.6)
    ._scope()
`} />

The harmonic series itself, played as a melody: 110, 220, 330 … 880 Hz. These are the exact frequencies hiding inside one sawtooth at 110 Hz.

## Where things live: the frequency map

Every part of a dance track owns a slice of the frequency range. Most mixing problems are two parts fighting for the same slice.

| Range | Name | What lives there |
|---|---|---|
| 20–60 Hz | Sub | Body of the kick, sub-bass sine. Felt more than heard. |
| 60–250 Hz | Bass | Bassline, kick punch, the "donk" |
| 250 Hz–2 kHz | Mids | Chords, stabs, vocals, hoover body |
| 2–6 kHz | Presence | Clap/snare snap, lead bite |
| 6–20 kHz | Air | Hi-hats, cymbals, noise risers |

A hi-hat is mostly **noise**: all frequencies at once, no harmonic spacing, so no pitch. A kick is mostly a **sine that drops in pitch fast** (from ~150 Hz to ~50 Hz in a few tens of milliseconds).

<Strudel title="sine kick vs noise hat" code={`
  stack(
    // kick: a sine whose pitch falls quickly
    note("a1*4").s("sine").penv(24).pdecay(0.06).decay(0.25).sustain(0),
    // hat: white noise, very short, high-passed
    s("~ white ~ white").decay(0.04).sustain(0).hpf(7000).gain(0.4)
  )._scope()
`} />
